Constitution Builder

Aristotle, Politics I and III · build a regime, then watch the scheme classify it

What you are about to do

Six decisions build a constitution. The page then classifies your creation using Aristotle's own criteria from Politics III.7 — how many rule, and in whose interest — and tells you which of his six regime types you have made.

After that, four real constitutions are put through the same scheme. You classify each one before you see the detail. One of the four cannot be classified at all, and finding out which is the point of the exercise.

Told in advance, as always. Most students build something recognisably modern and are then surprised by the label Aristotle's scheme attaches to it. The surprise is the teaching, not a trick: his democracy is a deviant form, and his polity is the one he admires. You are not being graded on which regime you build.

Build · stage 1 of 3

Aristotle's verdict on your constitution

The prior question you were not asked

He who has the power to take part in the deliberative or judicial administration of any state is said by us to be a citizen of that state. Politics III.1 (Jowett trans., 1885)

Notice the circularity. A citizen is one who shares in office; the constitution decides who shares in office; so the constitution decides who the citizens are, and the question “do the many rule?” cannot be answered until you already know who has been counted. Hold on to this. It is what makes case 2 below so difficult.

Verdict · stage 2 of 3

Four real constitutions

For each one: how many rule, and in whose interest? Choose, then the case opens and you can see what the scheme did or did not manage. Work through all four before moving on.

Stress test · stage 3 of 3

What you did

The two questions for Thursday

One. Switzerland before 1971 and South Africa after 1983 both held elections, had courts and constitutions, and both excluded most adult residents from the franchise — women in the first case, Black Africans in the second. Nearly everyone classifies them differently. State the principled difference. If you cannot state one, say so plainly: that is a more useful contribution than inventing a criterion on the spot.

Two. The 1960 Constitution of Cyprus has no place in Aristotle's six types, because his scheme assumes a single citizen body divided by wealth or virtue, not two communities each partly governing itself under external guarantee. Name the concept Aristotle would have needed. Then ask the harder version: is the gap a defect in a scheme built for the polis, or does it tell you something about the 1960 arrangement itself?

For the many, of whom each individual is not a good man, when they meet together may be better than the few good. Politics III.11 (Jowett trans., 1885)

Read III.11 against Module 2. Plato's answer to “who decides?” was the one who knows. Aristotle's is that assembled mediocrity may out-judge isolated excellence. That disagreement is the spine of the first half of this course, and you now have a regime of your own design to test it on.

Result

Instructor view

Session shape, Tuesday single period

The build and verdict take about eight minutes, the four cases about fifteen. On a single 50-minute Tuesday, run build and verdict in class, set the four cases as the asynchronous half, and open Thursday's double with the two questions. If you have the double period, run the whole thing and debrief in the second half.

Collect this on the board

Hands up for each of the six verdicts from stage 2. Expect a heavy clustering on democracy and polity, with a few aristocracies from students who gave offices to the educated. The oligarchies are usually accidental — a property qualification chosen for stability without noticing what it does to the interest column. Ask those students whether they intended an oligarchy. The honest ones say no, which is the opening for III.8 on how oligarchy and democracy are defined by wealth rather than by numbers.

Debrief order

Case 1 first, briefly: Athens is the paradigm and students mostly get it, though they resist calling it a deviation. Worth saying aloud that Aristotle wrote this classification as a resident alien in that city, with no vote and no right to own land — he is classifying the regime that hosted and excluded him.

Then Switzerland and South Africa as a pair, and do not let them be taken separately. This is the hour's hinge. Almost every student calls Switzerland rule of the many in the common interest and South Africa rule of the few in their own interest, and the two cases are structurally parallel on the franchise question. Push until someone produces one of three answers: that the excluded group was a minority in one case and a majority in the other (check the numbers — women were not a minority); that one system was open to reform from within and the other was not; or that the exclusion was by ascriptive group in one case and by sex in the other, which is a distinction that needs defending rather than stating. Any of the three is progress. The reflex answer — that one was a real democracy and the other a sham — is the thing to interrogate.

Cyprus 1960 last, and do not resolve it. The point is structural, not political: the scheme fails because it has one demos where the constitution had two. Students from either community will reach for familiar positions about who wrecked it; redirect to the classificatory question, which is genuinely neutral and genuinely hard.

If the room resists the Athens verdict

They will say democracy cannot be a deviation. Read III.7 aloud: the deviation is rule of the many in the interest of the needy rather than of the whole. Then ask whether any modern democracy satisfies the common-interest condition, and whether a scheme that classifies every actual popular government as deviant is diagnosing or merely disapproving.

Texts to have open

Politics III.1 (definition of the citizen), III.7 (the six types), III.8 (oligarchy and democracy by wealth), III.11 (the assembled many). Jowett, Gutenberg 6762, gutenberg.org/files/6762/6762-h/6762-h.htm. Book I.2 if the room needs reminding that the polis is prior to the individual, and I.4–7 if anyone wants to argue the hierarchy is detachable.
