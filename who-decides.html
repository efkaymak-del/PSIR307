PSIR 307 · History of Political Thought I · Week 2

Who Decides?

Plato, Republic VI–VII · four cases of expert authority against popular judgement

What this activity does, stated plainly

You will read four real cases in which a body of experts decided something, or displaced somebody who had been elected, or had its plan put to a vote. For each case you answer two separate questions: whether the arrangement was legitimate, and whether you agree with the substance of what was decided.

No hidden test. At the end, this page calculates the correlation between your two sets of answers — that is, whether your judgements about legitimacy move together with your agreement about outcomes. For most people they move together quite closely. You are being told this now, before you answer, because a gotcha would teach you nothing except to distrust the instrument. Answer as you actually think.

Four cases, two questions each, then one written sentence. About twelve minutes.

Privacy. Nothing is transmitted from this page: your answers stay in this browser tab and are not stored anywhere. At the end you copy a result line and paste it into the Week 2 Microsoft Form, which does go to a university system under EMU's tenancy. No name or student-number field appears here, because the Form identifies you through your institutional sign-in. Reloading this page clears your answers.

Why we did this to Plato

The argument of Republic VII is that authority should belong to those who know. Nobody in this room rejects that argument outright — you all want the surgeon chosen for competence. The difficulty is that the argument needs a way of identifying knowledge that does not reduce to agreeing with the conclusion. If your two columns move together, then in practice your test for “they were entitled to decide” is “they decided what I would have decided” — which is not a test of expertise at all, and which the philosopher-ruler argument cannot survive.

Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes. Republic VII, 517a (Jowett trans., 1892)

Notice also what Socrates does at 504e, before any of the famous images: asked what the Good is, he refuses to say, and offers a likeness instead. The institution in Book VII is built on a knowledge whose possessor declines to define it. Hold that against your own correlation figure and the week's question is in front of you.

Coda — one sentence, required

Submission

Instructor view

Sequence, 50-minute session

Minutes 0–3: state the disclosure aloud as well as on screen — they must know the instrument measures them before they use it. Minutes 3–15: four cases, individually, phones or laptops. Minutes 15–20: hands up by correlation band, write the distribution on the board. Minutes 20–40: debrief below. Minutes 40–45: live poll. Minutes 45–50: exit ticket.

Debrief, in order

Start with Case 3, not Case 1. Germany is the case that breaks the frame: the government conceded that the question was not one that reactor engineers could settle, and then appointed a commission anyway. Ask what the commission was expert in. If the answer is “values, not facts,” the Platonic claim has been conceded in form and emptied in substance, and that is the hinge of the hour.

Then Cases 1 and 2 together: Italy is expertise installed, Greece is expertise prevailing over an explicit vote. Students with high correlations usually split these two, which gives you a live example of the pattern without anyone having to confess.

Case 4 last. Expect the room's correlation to be highest here and expect that to be uncomfortable. Do not resolve it. The point is that a legitimacy judgement which tracks the outcome cannot do the work Book VII needs it to do, whichever way the student voted.

If the room's correlations come out low

Some cohorts, especially one that has done PSIR 308, answer strategically because they can see the instrument. Do not treat this as failure: ask them directly whether they separated the two judgements or performed separating them, and whether they could tell the difference from the inside. That question is better than the result.

Live poll cue, minute 40

Teams Forms, respondent names not recorded, launched inside the meeting so results stay hidden. Item 1 repeats the Case 4 legitimacy question on the same five-point scale, to compare with the entry ticket. Item 2: did your answer change, yes or no. Item 3, if yes: because of the argument, or because you saw the room? Ninety seconds, then close item 1 before discussion resumes.

Texts to have open

Republic 504e–509c and 514a–521b, Jowett, at gutenberg.org/files/1497/1497-h/1497-h.htm. The compulsion passage at 519d –520a is the one to read aloud if the room concludes too comfortably that experts should simply be left to it.
